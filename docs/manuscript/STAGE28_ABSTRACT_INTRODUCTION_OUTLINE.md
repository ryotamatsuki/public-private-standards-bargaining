**Candidate title:** *Agenda Rights, Sunk Compatibility Investment, and Public Standardization*

**Status:** PRE-MANUSCRIPT English text, *not* the LaTeX manuscript or a publication-ready proof. Internal theorem labels must be reconciled in Stage29. The baseline primitives, voting institutions, and all Stage11–27 proofs are unchanged. No claim of exhaustive literature priority.

## Abstract (draft, 174 words)

International standardization may require governments to act after firms have made irreversible investments in partially compatible technologies. We study how the distribution of government proposal rights interacts with voting requirements and endogenous private investment in a three-country oligopoly. Each national firm chooses whether to develop an openly usable technology and, if so, two continuous design attributes. Governments subsequently decide whether to mandate a preexisting public standard. In a parameter region where a privately developed two-firm regional standard and the public standard coexist under unanimity, we characterize pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibrium outcomes over all firms' admissible investments, including heterogeneous designs. With a one- or two-vote approval threshold, public standardization is the only pure-equilibrium terminal outcome if and only if at least two governments hold proposal rights. Unanimity instead preserves regional-standard equilibria. The institutional shift need not improve aggregate welfare. Three distinct thresholds govern the outsider government's veto, the welfare effect of a retrofit after private investment, and the welfare ranking of initial standardization allocations. These results hold for a deliberately restricted open-technology game and do not characterize mixed equilibria or proprietary standards.

**Keywords:** Compatibility standards; agenda setting; international coordination; irreversibility; standard-setting institutions; oligopoly.

**JEL (provisional):** L15, L13, F13, D72.

## 1. Introduction — draft

Compatibility standards create a coordination problem that is simultaneously technical, commercial, and political. A common technical interface can expand the effective network available to users, but adopting that interface may also alter the competitive positions of firms whose products were developed around different designs. A national government therefore evaluates a proposed standard not only through its effects on consumers but also through the rents earned by its domestic producer. Those incentives need not agree across countries. Moreover, the institutional authority to place a regulatory proposal on the agenda is logically distinct from the number of national approvals required to adopt it. A standard may have enough potential supporters to pass a vote and nevertheless never be proposed.

The central question of this paper is how that distinction interacts with private technological investment. Suppose firms must commit resources to a compatibility architecture before they know whether governments will subsequently mandate a public alternative. If compatibility investments are partly irreversible, the firms' ex ante response to a prospective national vote can differ sharply from the governments' ex post preferences after the investment is observed. To understand which standards can actually persist, one must analyze both the political decision and the entire preceding technology-investment game. A government may prefer not to replace a privately installed standard at a particular history, but that history need not be supportable by firms' optimal initial investments.

We construct a deliberately parsimonious three-country model to separate these effects. Each country hosts a firm, and the three firms compete in differentiated-product Cournot markets. At the first stage, every firm either undertakes no development or chooses an openly usable private technological design. A design has two continuous dimensions, one associated with compatibility-related productivity and the other with commonality that changes competitive substitution; the effective bilateral attributes are given by the minima of participating firms' investments. Investment requires a fixed setup cost and convex physical capital expenditures, paid before any government decision. In particular, a privately chosen technological attribute cannot subsequently be recovered simply because its owner is required to comply with a different standard.

After observing the full investment profile, governments may consider a single legally available public interface. Each national government has a domestic-welfare objective: national firm profit plus consumer surplus in its market. The constitutional rule specifies two distinct rights. The *agenda set* identifies the governments that may independently propose a public mandate; the *approval quorum* identifies the number of affirmative national votes needed to enact it. We compare one-, two-, and three-vote quorums and nonempty subsets of agenda-authorized governments. If the mandate is enacted, firms decide whether to comply. A firm already using the open technological family need only complete the missing capital stock; it neither pays a second fixed setup charge nor recovers the costs of a design element made redundant by the mandate. We solve the induced policy and compliance subgames after every possible initial profile and examine pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibria of the full sequential game.

The main result is a sharp *institutional* threshold in a certified region of technology and investment costs. When one or two affirmative votes suffice, the only pure-equilibrium terminal standard is the public interface if and only if at least two governments are authorized to place the mandate on the agenda. With only one agenda holder, a privately optimal bilateral standard can persist when that agenda holder is the excluded third country. The initial no-development profile followed by mandatory public standardization also remains an equilibrium. Under unanimity, all three possible bilateral founding coalitions can sustain a private regional standard, regardless of the distribution of proposal rights. These are statements about *terminal outcomes of pure-strategy equilibria*. They do not imply that investment choices are unique or that mixed equilibria have been excluded.

The number two in this result has an elementary institutional component. Any two-government agenda set intersects every potential two-government coalition that would approve a mandate. That combinatorial observation alone is not our contribution. The substantive equilibrium problem is to prove that all investment profiles which could survive privately while avoiding the public mandate either generate a sufficient approving coalition or admit a profitable private investment deviation. This issue cannot be resolved simply by tabulating votes at an assumed symmetric regional standard. Individual firms choose both continuous attributes independently; private histories may contain zero, one, two, or three investors, with heterogeneous bilateral technological compatibility. We establish the result by eliminating redundant private investment maxima, distinguishing low- from high-commonality investment decisions, and characterizing the governments' welfare comparisons for the remaining asymmetric three-investor configurations. The argument rules out private terminal pure equilibria without assuming symmetric initial technology choices.

The existence of an exact institutional threshold does not imply that extensive use of proposal rights is desirable. We separate three economically different benchmarks in evaluating an observed two-firm privately developed standard. First, the excluded government's *veto threshold* compares the gains from a public mandate with the new costs that its previously noninvesting national firm must incur. Second, the *ex post welfare threshold* compares continuing with the privately developed standard against imposing a public retrofit after private investment has become sunk. Third, the *ex ante welfare threshold* compares the initial feasible allocations: developing and retaining the regional standard versus developing no private standard and adopting the public interface from the outset. We show that these thresholds are strictly ordered in a region with positive private investment. The ex ante and ex post world-welfare thresholds differ by exactly the nonrecoverable cost of the private commonality attribute.

This wedge matters for constitutional comparisons. There is a parameter region in which universal public compatibility would have been socially preferable *before* firms developed a regional standard, yet a mandate requiring them to retrofit *afterward* would destroy aggregate welfare. There is also a region in which the privately maintained regional standard has higher ex ante world welfare than the public equilibrium obtained when the agenda right is expanded. The outsider government's preferred vote, the efficiency of ex post regulatory intervention, and the welfare ranking of alternative constitutional equilibrium allocations are therefore distinct objects. In particular, a constitutional reform that eliminates all regional-standard pure equilibria is not automatically a welfare-improving reform.

Our analysis is related to several strands of the literature, and it is important to delimit its contribution carefully. Gandal and Shy (2001) already examine national standard recognition and three-country standardization unions, showing that two countries can have incentives to exclude a third under sufficiently high conversion costs. Klimenko (2009) analyzes strategic government compatibility policies under network externalities and international competition and demonstrates noncooperative policy distortions. Costinot (2008) compares international regulatory agreements for compatibility standards and shows that the welfare ranking of national treatment and mutual recognition changes with the intensity of network effects. Accordingly, neither the coexistence of regional and international standardization nor an institutional reversal of welfare rankings can be claimed as new. What differs in the present environment is the separate statutory right to propose a common *public* mandate, the national approval quorum, and the endogenous pure-equilibrium sustainability of a previously sunk, continuously chosen *open* technical investment.

The paper also builds on the standard-setting and innovation literature. Farrell and Saloner (1988) compare standards committees, markets, and preemptive action. Farrell and Simcoe (2012) analyze a standards-setting war of attrition, showing how neutral intervention and procedure design interact with screening and ex ante proposal improvement. The latter paper explicitly discusses a two-vote rule as an implementation of neutral intervention. Ruiz (2004) endogenizes firms' product characteristics in a model of governments' foreign-standard recognition decisions. These contributions preclude the broad claims that a voting rule, an intervention right, or endogenous product differentiation is novel in itself. Our narrower focus is a sequential *national constitutional* game in which costly, individually chosen compatibility stocks can be retrospectively displaced by a legally available common public interface. The relevant difference is a global classification of the *pure-SPE terminal outcome set* over those private investments, not the mere presence of strategic standard-setting.

The result is intentionally conditional on a restricted technological and institutional environment. The public interface is assumed to preexist and to be legally implementable; the private technical family is open rather than patented, and no firm can negotiate a licensing fee or political side payment. The government tie-breaking convention and the exact voting sequence are specified. Our theorem establishes a nonempty region of primitive parameters and an independently verified local robustness neighborhood, not a complete characterization of every conceivable network economy or every international voting constitution. In particular, broader technological ownership, an endogenous public reference, and mixed strategies remain outside the analysis.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the technology, capital-cost, and Cournot environments. Section 3 derives firms' mandatory-compliance incentives and governments' policy subgames. Section 4 establishes the institutional threshold and characterizes which privately developed regional standards survive the full initial-investment game. Section 5 distinguishes national veto and the two welfare benchmarks, and Section 6 summarizes robustness and the model's institutional limitations. The Appendix provides the full continuous-investment proofs, the asymmetric three-investor case classification, and exact rational verification certificates.

## Main text proposition candidates (not final numbering)

**Proposition A — Agenda coverage and terminal pure-SPE outcomes.** In the baseline parameter rectangle, for any q∈{1,2} and any nonempty agenda-authorized subset A of the three governments, every pure-strategy SPE final outcome is public mandated IS iff |A|>=2. If |A|=1, the complementary bilateral O(1,a_R*(eta)) private RS exists as a pure SPE alongside NNN/M. For q=3, all three regional pairs and public NNN/M exist as pure SPE.

**Proposition B — Investment deterrence and global reduction.** The publicly favorable national coalition need not appear at all private histories. But any putative initial L-pure-SPE technology profile with a unique maximum a or (conditional on L and tied max a) a unique maximum c admits a strict private deviation. Every remaining possible OON or OOO pure terminal L design has either an approving public coalition that the agenda set intersects or a profitable unilateral lower-a design deviation.

**Proposition C — Political veto and sunk-capital efficiency thresholds.** Along the bilaterally privately optimal OON technical design, let K_V, K_E, K_W be the outsider's national-veto, ex post retrofit social efficiency, and ex ante public-versus-private allocation world-welfare cutoffs. Then K_V<K_E<K_W and K_W−K_E=phi*a_R*(eta)^2. At K=.55, certified crossings are eta_E∈(.111,.112) and eta_W∈(.118,.119). The exact middle wedge separates ex ante desirability of public IS from ex post desirability of forcing public retrofit after sunk RS investment.

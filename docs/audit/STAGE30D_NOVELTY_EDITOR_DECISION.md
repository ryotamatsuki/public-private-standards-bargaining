## 1. What has changed since the original NO-GO

The original Stage29 paper promoted two largely general observations—any winning coalition needs an eligible proposer; sunk design costs separate ex-ante from ex-post accounts. The Stage30 adverse referee was therefore defensible. The Stage30B/C mathematics now certifies an economically different object:
- exact correspondence of **all** private pure investment SPEs with endogenous outsider-protected bilateral coordination (C1);
- exact public-SPE welfare despite different initial investments and detailed full initial public correspondence for a broad agenda (C2–C3);
- maximum private equilibrium welfare over all private designs plus reform's all-old-equilibria welfare guarantee and best/worst tradeoff (C5–C6);
- producer/home-government/outsider misalignment and K-only political filter as explanatory mechanisms (C4/C7).

The novel **candidate** is joint rather than seven independent pieces: a political public fallback modifies which weakest-link standards are privately sustainable, and agenda expansion deletes this set; the distribution of welfare over *all* pre-reform private equilibria determines whether the change can be certified as no worse than any prior pure SPE.

The result could yield a publishable full paper in applied IO/public economics if priority claims hold and the model-specific institutional primitives are defended. Novelty by "no earlier article contains exactly these six equations" is not adequate.

## 2. The decisive new threat from THIS Stage6 pass

**Takarada, Kawabata, Yanase & Kurata (2020), JPET 22,1420–1441**, published Proposition 5 and Proposition 6, analyze **three-country** multilateral versus regional standards **and outsider blocking** through a coalition core; their paper also explicitly discusses fixed costs. Even a globally welfare-maximizing multilateral standard can fail to enter the core, while a regional arrangement survives. Consequently, outsider veto and under-coordination of the globally desirable standard are **not** independent current-paper novelties. Attributing a generic regional-vs-global welfare conflict to our new model as a discovery would be materially misleading.

**Bordignon & Brusco (2006), JPubE 90,2063–2090**, especially 2003 WP Propositions 5–7, analyze initial standards chosen in anticipation of future harmonization and the impact of special-subgroup institutional rules on centralization. They independently eliminate the credibility of the broad claim that preharmonization standard choices and political integration were not studied together before.

Nevertheless neither known source, **under the verified form we could inspect**, contains a distinct **private corporation and its national government** with opposite preferences, a firm-to-public \`N\` withdrawal fallback, and all-real corporate weakest-link Cournot investment first-stage SPE sets. Takarada's solution is the **core of country coalitions**, not subgame-perfect Nash equilibria of separate investing firms and subsequently voting governments. Bordignon's investing players are **governments selecting national standards**, not privately motivated firm investors. The failure to find a mapping that preserves both payoff operators is a substantive, not a terminological distinction; it is not enough, however, to certify the new paper's journal priority while the full published proofs are inaccessible.

## 4. Provisional Contribution Completeness and editorial test

**Field-editor case beyond correctness (English, for later independent author revision):**

> Standardization policy can alter private technology investment long before a public mandate is adopted. We show how private coordination, an outside country's deferred adoption cost, and national agenda rights jointly determine which partially compatible private standards are sustained in pure-strategy equilibrium. Firms maintaining a regional standard weakly prefer to keep it, while their own governments strictly prefer the public interface; political protection instead comes from a noninvesting outsider. We characterize the entire private-equilibrium correspondence and show when sufficiently dispersed proposal rights eliminate it. We then evaluate the change without selecting a favorable private equilibrium: although all public equilibria have identical welfare, private equilibria differ substantially in welfare. We identify the exact condition under which the reform does not lower welfare relative to any old pure equilibrium, and the region where it removes both low- and high-welfare regional standards. The results are deliberately conditional on a specified three-country open-interface model and prescribed sincere government voting.

**Strongest pro-publication argument:** The paper answers a real field-specific comparison absent from narrow ex-post standards-voting exercises: conditional on a mandated legally available public technology, policy rights alter not merely an ex-post vote but the **entire endogenous private investment equilibrium set**, and thereby the **welfare-support function** of constitutional choice. This is more than a different parametrization of the agenda intersection lemma.

**Strongest desk-reject argument:** Weakest-link contribution continua (Cornes–Hartley), outside options (Grossklags et al.), voting and investment (Harstad), outsider regional veto (Takarada et al.), and three-country standards over time (Bordignon–Brusco) have all been studied. Given the public interface is exogenously free, its mandate-specific cost advantage, the discontinuous two- vs three-developer demand and mandated sincere voting, the new signs may amount to a calibrated 2D Cournot verification of a transparent conjunction of old mechanisms. Worse, the published-theorem corpora for several critical papers have not been read. **The full-paper case would fail if a broader parent theorem directly characterizes politically filtered minimum investment games or if publisher versions contain the same all-equilibrium support comparison.**

**Current editor conclusion:** The **remaining joint insight has enough plausibility to justify a single bounded published-priority closure**, but not a declaration of novelty PASS, full-paper GO, or a new research Stage to generate more variants. It is stronger than Stage29's original article, but source uncertainty prevents an unconditional result.

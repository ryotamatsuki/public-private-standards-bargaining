## Working abstract (≈165 words; provisional and conditional)

Can sunk investments by private firms make a common public standard politically unattainable even when the governments of those firms favor it? We study a three-country market in which firms choose whether and how extensively to invest in a shared private technology before governments may require adoption of a pre-existing common interface. A country's willingness to adopt depends on domestic consumer surplus and its firm's profits; the ability to propose the mandate is institutionally restricted. We characterize the entire pure-strategy investment equilibrium correspondence within a specified compatibility-family benchmark. Private standards are sustained by two investing firms, but the government that protects them is the noninvesting outsider seeking to avoid a new implementation setup cost. A sufficiently broad proposal regime eliminates the private equilibria. Yet uniform public adoption is not an unconditional welfare improvement. Public pure equilibria share a common world-welfare level, whereas private investment equilibria can lie on either side of it. We derive the condition under which a reform weakly improves world welfare relative to every pre-reform pure equilibrium and identify its best-versus-worst equilibrium welfare trade-off.

**Abstract scope tag** in eventual finalized version: the demand category is discrete and the government vote/proposal rule is sincere and stipulated; no claim of general-voting SPE, formal theorem firstness or empirical applicability.

## Working Introduction (English, author-facing provisional argument; ~1,150 words)

Technical standards are often treated either as private coordination devices or as objects of public harmonization. These perspectives are related but need not coincide. Firms can invest in a shared technical architecture to improve their commercial positions, while public authorities may subsequently prefer a different, already available interface because they also value consumer surplus. The private investments that make a standard profitable need not make the national governments of its developers politically committed to preserving it. If adopting a common interface requires countries that have not invested to incur new costs, the political support for a private standard may instead come from outside the group of firms that established it.

This paper studies a question arising from that separation: **who protects a privately developed standard when its founding firms and founding governments disagree?** We also ask a second, closely linked question: when an institutional reform removes the political obstacles to a common public standard, does it raise world welfare independently of which private investment equilibrium would otherwise have emerged? Answering both questions requires tracking firms' incentives *before* governments take a standards-policy decision. An ex post vote alone reveals whether a proposed common standard would pass, but it does not identify which private investment profiles can be sustained when firms anticipate that vote.

We examine a three-country oligopoly with one firm associated with each country. Firms initially decide whether to join an open technology family, paying an irreversible basic qualification cost, and choose two continuous additional design investments. Shared bilateral functionality is constrained by the lower investment of the partners. After observing those investments, governments can mandate a previously specified common public interface. Whether that policy is enacted depends on a nationally evaluated welfare gain, the required number of affirmative votes, and which governments are eligible to propose it. Product-market competition then takes place in all countries. Each government values the worldwide operating profit of its nationally owned firm and consumer surplus in its own market; its preferences therefore differ from those of the firm.

Two features of the environment matter particularly for the political incidence of compatibility. First, private developers can reuse their basic qualification expense if a public mandate is adopted. A firm that has not joined the open family, by contrast, must bear a new setup expense to comply. Second, adoption decisions are governed by a stipulated sincere national-welfare proposal-and-vote rule. This rule allows us to isolate how institutional proposal rights and voting thresholds reshape *investment-stage* incentives. It is not intended to characterize every Nash continuation of an unrestricted simultaneous political voting game.

The first result gives a complete characterization of privately sustained pure-strategy equilibria. In the certified parameter region, every such equilibrium consists, up to relabeling, of two firms selecting the same positive-category private standard design and a third firm remaining outside. The entire continuum of stable bilateral investments is characterized by three restrictions: a marginal investment condition, a founding firm's participation condition relative to the public fallback, and a strict political veto condition for the outsider government. The result is an if-and-only-if characterization over the original continuous strategy space, not merely a symmetric candidate or a locally optimal investment.

The political configuration behind these equilibria is striking. The two firms that developed the private standard weakly prefer it to the public mandate. Nevertheless, **their national governments strictly favor** replacing it with the common interface. The **noninvesting outsider government strictly opposes** the change, because mandatory entry into the common family would require a fresh setup expenditure. The political protector of the private standard is thus a country that did not develop it, rather than either government whose firm did. This is a cost-incidence mechanism: investment makes private status-quo production privately attractive, while having already paid the qualifying expense makes public conversion less costly for the founding countries than for the outsider.

Political institutions feed back into that private investment equilibrium. Under unanimity, or under a nonunanimous voting threshold when only the outsider may propose a mandate, private standards can persist. Under a rule requiring no more than two affirmative votes and granting proposal rights to at least two governments, no private-terminal pure equilibrium remains in the certified parameter region. Firms foresee that the founding governments will support a public mandate and cannot rely on the outsider to block its proposal. The initial pure-equilibrium profiles in the latter regime need not be unique, but all lead to the public interface. The change is therefore not only a post-investment voting outcome; it eliminates a whole class of privately sustainable *earlier* technology choices.

The welfare effects of that institutional change are more subtle than its adoption effects. All public-terminal pure equilibria generate the same world welfare because the initial investments retained on public paths carry no wasted additional commonality expense. By contrast, the privately sustained investment set contains multiple designs with different welfare consequences. We characterize the welfare-maximizing design **within that entire private pure-equilibrium set**, rather than selecting an arbitrary private design or solving a planner's unrestricted technology problem. Combining the private welfare maximum with public equilibrium welfare yields a sharp condition: a reform that replaces a private-admitting constitution with a broad-proposal regime weakly improves world welfare relative to **every** original pure equilibrium exactly when the mandatory setup cost does not exceed a specified cutoff.

Outside this guarantee region, constitutional reform can improve the worst equilibrium while eliminating the best attainable private one. This is not a consequence of changing the parameters across different examples: at the same market parameters, one privately sustainable design can lie below the public outcome and another above it. Thus success in enforcing a common interface does not determine a welfare ranking when the alternative private standard has multiple equilibrium designs. The appropriate object of comparison is a *set of equilibrium welfare outcomes*, not simply one private outcome against one public outcome. The institutional implication is about what a rule change can guarantee without assuming an equilibrium-selection mechanism; it is not an expected-welfare prediction or a national Pareto claim.

Our analysis connects three strands of research. Work on compatibility and network markets endogenizes firm choices and studies how compatibility costs affect equilibria and welfare (e.g., Buccella, Fanti, and Gori, 2023). Work on national product standards examines regulatory harmonization, outsiders' objections and coalition stability (e.g., Costinot, 2008; Takarada et al., 2020), while work on standard-setting institutions and political rules shows that procedures can change investments undertaken before a collective decision (e.g., Harstad, 2005; Farrell and Simcoe, 2012). These mechanisms are well established individually. Our contribution is the **combined equilibrium object**: independent private firms invest before *distinct national governments* use proposal rights and welfare criteria to decide whether to displace their standard, and we trace the consequences through the entire pure investment equilibrium correspondence and its welfare support. We do not claim to have invented compatibility games, outsider vetoes, institutional investment effects or fixed-cost welfare cutoffs.

The results deliberately concern a transparent benchmark rather than an actual proposed treaty. In the private market, basic technology-family qualification is a discrete action supported by a setup cost, whereas the two further investment stocks determine incremental functionality and positioning. A third firm's entry can therefore affect consumer perceptions of the category even when it adds zero *incremental* capacity. This category-contingent demand effect is **assumed as a reduced form**, not derived from a general model of technology compatibility. Likewise, the government rule prescribes sincere proposal and voting behavior; the public interface is already available without extra government drafting expense; and the complete characterization has been proved only in a specified two-parameter region holding the other market coefficients fixed. We state these restrictions at the outset because the conclusions need not extend to alternative demand systems, unrefined strategic voting or positive public implementation costs.

The remainder of the paper defines the technology and political decision rule, characterizes all privately sustained investment equilibria, establishes the effects of agenda access on public equilibrium selection, compares the entire constitutional equilibrium welfare sets, and discusses the boundaries of the mechanism.

## Two result summaries for eventual Main Contributions subsection

**Result 1 — Outsider-protected private coordination (C1+C3+C7).** In the certified benchmark, only two matching private developers and one noninvesting outsider can support a private-terminal pure SPE. Privately developing firms weakly prefer retaining their standard, although their own governments strictly prefer the public one. The outsider's new adoption expense creates its strictly opposed governmental position. With a sufficiently broad proposer set and nonunanimous quorum, no private-terminal pure SPE survives, and all public pure equilibria have initial design choices N or O(c_i,0).

**Result 2 — Welfare assurance over all old/new pure equilibria (C2+C5+C6).** The public interface produces identical world welfare at every public-terminal pure SPE; the old private equilibrium welfare varies with two-dimensional design choices. The private support set has a globally characterized welfare maximum at (1,a*(eta)). The government institution reform is weakly welfare-dominant against **every** old pure equilibrium iff K<=K_W(eta); above the cutoff it raises the equilibrium welfare infimum but decreases its supremum. The social benefit of uniformity and the welfare guarantee against endogenous private adaptation are separate.

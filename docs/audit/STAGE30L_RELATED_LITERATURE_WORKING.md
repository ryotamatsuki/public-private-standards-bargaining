## Working Related Literature section (English, provisional ~750 words)

Our analysis is related first to the economics of private compatibility decisions, where network effects, product differentiation and the costs of using a shared technical architecture interact. Foundational work by Katz and Shapiro (1985) establishes the importance of compatibility and network demand for industry incentives. More closely, Buccella, Fanti, and Gori (2023) consider a Cournot compatibility-decision game and show how quasi-fixed compatibility costs change the existence, multiplicity and welfare rankings of compatible and incompatible subgame-perfect equilibria. This literature shows why fixed-cost-dependent standards equilibria and changes in their social ranking are not novel on their own. Our object is different: private compatibility investments also affect the subsequent national mandate decision, and the surviving *complete* set of first-stage pure equilibria depends on which governments may put that proposal to a vote. The government’s payoff includes domestic consumer surplus, so it need not agree with its own firm's private compatibility decision.

Studies of standard-setting organizations also link institutions to private technological choices. Nabin, Sgro, and Lahiri (2023) show that a standard-setting organization's presence can change firms' quality, process innovation, technological gaps and industry welfare. Chou and Bandera (2020) compare compatible and partially incompatible messaging protocols in an oligopoly game and discuss government action favoring common standards. We do not claim to be the first to consider firms choosing standards or to compare public and private standards. Instead, our institutional decision is evaluated after firms have irrevocably selected continuous private design investments; governments have separate objectives and proposal eligibility rather than a single exogenously imposed regulator. The central question is which private investments remain an equilibrium when the later public decision is rationally anticipated.

A second group of papers analyzes national standards and international harmonization. Gandal and Shy (2001), Barrett and Yang (2001), and Klimenko (2009) examine different sources of strategic incompatibility, mandatory compatibility and national incentives. In particular, Barrett and Yang show that the incentives of domestic incumbents and governments to reject international standards can depend on pre-existing technology and costly adjustment. Costinot (2008) distinguishes the welfare consequences of national treatment and mutual recognition in international product standards; Takarada, Kawabata, Yanase, and Kurata (2020) examine the political stability of regional and multilateral mandatory standards using the core. The latter establishes that a globally beneficial harmonization arrangement may be blocked by a nonmember government. We therefore do not treat outsider opposition to multilateral standards as an independent discovery. Our difference is to allow *private firms* to choose sunk family investments before *distinct home-country governments* compare public adoption with the installed private alternatives; the outsider veto is an endogenously relevant constraint on the firms' entire earlier investment equilibrium correspondence.

Bordignon and Brusco (2006) analyze intergovernmental enhanced cooperation and the future prospects for harmonization, illustrating that the institutional treatment of regional agreements can change later integration prospects. That work also warns against describing our policy timing or cooperation externality as intrinsically new. The economically distinct hypothesis we investigate is that the firms whose private standard is threatened are not the governments that preserve it: under the model's actual investment and setup-cost accounting, the founders' home governments favor a public interface, whereas a country with no private investment protects the status quo against a fresh mandated setup cost. This distribution of political incentives is central to the private equilibrium existence test.

A third literature considers prepolicy investment and collective decision-making rules. Harstad (2005) analyzes how majority requirements alter prior investment incentives. Farrell and Simcoe (2012) show how decision procedures in consensus standardization influence incentives to improve proposals. Continuous minimum-contribution games, including Cornes and Hartley (2007) and Barbieri and Malueg (2019), generate equilibrium multiplicity and coordination problems that resemble the weakest-link element of our bilateral shared design. We build on these precedents rather than claim that agenda rules affect investment or that minimum-contribution games generate multiple equilibria for the first time. The distinctive object here is the *politically filtered investment correspondence* under a specified national-welfare selector, including firms' deviations that change the political continuation and trigger new private-family participation.

Our welfare comparison follows that distinction. The literature establishes that compatibility equilibria can have different social values and that national standard regimes can differ in stability and efficiency. We instead consider the world-welfare **support of all pure equilibria** under alternative proposal constitutions. We first prove which private investment profiles are sustainable and the maximal welfare obtainable among them; we then give a condition for reform to weakly dominate every pre-reform pure equilibrium without assuming which old equilibrium firms would have selected. Where the condition fails, the reform can raise worst-case equilibrium welfare while losing the best private outcome. This is a selection-robust diagnostic for the present benchmark, not a claim of unique selected equilibria, a general optimal voting system or an empirical forecast of actual standards legislation.

## Claim-vs-source falsification questions (pre-publication)

1. Does a published parent derive *exactly* a strategic firm's first-stage investment set under a **later** distinct-government mandatory standard policy selector, or does it only take harmonization as exogenous/coalition-core? Distinguish both.
2. Could the founder government/outsider opposite sign merely be a relabeling of an existing single-veto government from Barrett–Yang/Takarada with no new investor adjustment? Need compare the strategy and information sets, NOT the prose labels alone.
3. If an existing general "minimum investments + endogenous government veto + prescribed proposal rights" theorem directly gives R and the welfare bounds, our article is a specialization; do NOT claim independent novelty.
4. Does a prior paper study welfare using **all equilibria** rather than one efficient/cooperative or selected SPE? Check scope and quantifier before differentiating.
5. Are national welfare preferences consistent with the paper's actual national ownership and firm sales across three markets? Do not compare domestic sales-only government model as identical.
6. Could C6's all-old/all-new inequality be independent economics *without* C1/C5? No. Show the proof bridge and the old low/high feasible private witnesses.
